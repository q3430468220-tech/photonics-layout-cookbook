Two waveguides brought close together exchange their light. A short stretch taps off a few per cent into the neighbour, and a longer one splits the light in half. This chapter draws a directional coupler with the PDK and finds the length that gives a 50:50 split at 1.55 µm. It also shows how far the split moves when the wavelength changes.

The work takes three steps: the cross-section first, then the length, then the finished device. Each step checks the one before it.

The device #

devices/directional_coupler_builder.py draws two strip waveguides, 0.5 µm wide, that run side by side over a straight section. An S-bend at each of the four ends takes them apart again. From the repository root:

shell
uv run python devices/directional_coupler_builder.py

coupler: gap 0.2 um over 15 um, S-bends 10 um long (tightest radius 11.744 um), ports 4 um apart
GDS -> devices/gds/directional_coupler.gds   ports: ['o1', 'o2', 'o3', 'o4']
DRC passed: 0 errors, 0 warnings
parameter default where it comes from
gap 0.20 µm the minimum space of the waveguide layer: the strongest coupling the process allows
length 15 µm step 2: the 50:50 length at 1.55 µm is 15.03 µm
port_spacing 4 µm centre to centre, at both ends
bend_length 10 µm each S-bend; its tightest radius, 11.7 µm, is above the 5 µm rule

Light enters at o1. The waveguide it stays in ends at o4, the through port. The other waveguide ends at o3, the cross port. The builder refuses a gap below the minimum space, and S-bends that curve tighter than the bend radius of the design rules.

The steps #

step folder solver script the question it answers
1 1_supermodes MODE (FDE) supermodes_fde.py How fast do two parallel waveguides exchange light, for a given gap?
2 2_coupling_length FDTD coupling_length_fdtd.py How long must the straight section be, with the bends counted in?
3 3_coupler_fdtd FDTD coupler_fdtd.py What does the device as drawn do across the band?

Run it #

You need Lumerical with MODE and FDTD. See the prerequisites of chapter 4. The run times are from the author's machine, with 16 threads.

shell
cd lumerical_simulations/directional_coupler/scripts
uv run python supermodes_fde.py                 # step 1, about 2 minutes
uv run python coupling_length_fdtd.py --preview # draws the layout, the simulation region and the ports
uv run python coupling_length_fdtd.py           # step 2, about 35 minutes for four lengths
uv run python coupler_fdtd.py                   # step 3, about 13 minutes
uv run python coupler_fdtd.py --mesh 0.01 --name coupler_fdtd_fine   # the mesh check of step 3, about 40 minutes

The figures are redrawn from the kept results, without Lumerical:

shell
uv run python lumerical_simulations/directional_coupler/figures/fig1_supermodes.py
uv run python lumerical_simulations/directional_coupler/figures/fig2_coupling_length.py
uv run python lumerical_simulations/directional_coupler/figures/fig3_coupler.py

Step 1. The supermodes, by MODE #

The question How long does it take two parallel strips, 0.2 µm apart, to pass their light across?

The idea. Two waveguides side by side do not have a mode each. Together they have two: the even supermode, with the same field in both cores, and the odd one, with opposite fields. Light that starts in one waveguide is the sum of the two. They travel at slightly different speeds, so along the coupler the sum moves across to the other waveguide, and back again. All of it has crossed after

L_π = λ / (2 (n_even − n_odd))

and half of it after Lπ / 2. One mode calculation on the cross-section therefore gives the scale of the device.

The model. A straight piece of the two strips, written to GDS and loaded into a Layer Builder with the process file. The mode solver uses a window of 6 µm by 2.4 µm with a 20 nm mesh, and 10 nm over both cores and across the gap, with a mesh line on every silicon surface.

The results (supermodes.txt):

quantity value
even supermode, 1.55 µm neff = 2.45591
odd supermode neff = 2.43528
difference 0.02064
cross-over length Lπ 37.55 µm
half of it 18.78 µm
gap 200 nm 250 nm 300 nm 350 nm 400 nm 500 nm
Lπ 37.6 µm 58.4 µm 90.3 µm 139 µm 214 µm 503 µm

Reading the figure.

  • a and b, the two supermodes. The even one has light in the gap; the odd one has a zero in the middle of it. More of the even mode is in silicon and oxide between the cores, so its index is higher. The single strip of the strip chapter has 2.4445, between the two.
  • c, the gap. Lπ grows by a factor e for every 116 nm of gap. The field of one waveguide falls off exponentially outside its core, and the coupling is the part of that tail that reaches the other core.
  • d, the wavelength. Lπ falls from 46.8 µm at 1500 nm to 30.3 µm at 1600 nm, by 35 %. At a longer wavelength the mode is wider and reaches further into the gap.

What it tells you. At the smallest gap the process allows, a 50:50 coupler is about 19 µm long. The gap is the sensitive dimension. By the fit of panel c, 10 nm more gap makes Lπ 9 % longer. The length is not sensitive in that way: a micrometre more or less is 3 % of Lπ.

Pitfall The two supermodes differ by 0.02 in index, and Lπ is the inverse of that difference. An error of 0.001 in one index moves Lπ by 5 %. Step 1 therefore meshes the gap finely and puts mesh lines on the surfaces. Both modes are found in the same run, on the same mesh, so their common errors cancel in the difference.

Step 2. The length, by FDTD #

The question How long must the straight section be for a 50:50 split, when the S-bends are part of the device?

The idea. The S-bends couple too. Where they approach the straight section, the two cores are already close. Step 1 knows nothing of that. FDTD on the whole layout includes it. The fraction of the light that crosses follows

cross / (cross + through) = sin²( π/2 · (L + L_bends) / L_π )

where L is the length of the straight section and Lbends is the length of straight coupler that the four bends are worth. Two unknowns need at least two lengths; four lengths check that the formula holds.

The model. The builder's coupler with a 3 µm lead at each port, for straight sections of 0, 6, 12 and 18 µm, each loaded into a Layer Builder. The FDTD region ends 1 µm before the ends of the leads, so the waveguides run on into the absorbing boundary. A mode port sits on each of the four waveguides; light enters at port 1. The mesh is the solver's automatic one (accuracy 2) with a 20 nm override over the gap, the straight section and 4 µm of the bends at each end.

Run --preview first. It draws the layout with the region and the ports, which is the quickest way to catch a port in the wrong place.

The results (coupling_length.txt):

straight section cross through sum fraction that crosses the fit
0 µm 0.0158 0.9835 0.9994 0.0158 0.0159
6 µm 0.1440 0.8546 0.9987 0.1442 0.1442
12 µm 0.3685 0.6309 0.9994 0.3687 0.3688
18 µm 0.6282 0.3706 0.9988 0.6290 0.6289
quantity value
Lπ from the fit 35.81 µm
Lbends, what the four bends are worth 2.88 µm
50:50 length, Lπ / 2 − Lbends 15.03 µm
Lπ from the supermodes of step 1 37.55 µm, 4.6 % longer

Reading the figure.

  • a, the fit. The four points lie on the sin² curve within 0.0001. With no straight section at all, 1.6 % already crosses, in the bends. The dashed curve is step 1 without the bends, and it lies to the right: it needs more length for the same split.
  • b, the wavelength. Every length crosses more light at longer wavelengths. A coupler is 50:50 at one wavelength.

What it tells you. The straight section must be 15.0 µm. A design from the cross-section alone would have taken Lπ / 2 = 18.8 µm from step 1, and by the fit of this step that coupler crosses 66 % of the light, not 50 %. The bends account for most of that difference and the 4.6 % in Lπ for the rest.

Checks.

  • Energy. Cross and through add up to 0.9987 or more in every run: the coupler loses under 0.006 dB. At most 6 × 10⁻⁵ of the power comes back at the input, and as little leaves at port 2.
  • The model. Two parameters fitted to four points leave no residual above 0.0001. The sin² law describes this device.
  • Against step 1. The two values of Lπ come from different solvers and differ by 4.6 %. Step 3 simulates the device at the fitted length, which is the test that matters.

Pitfalls.

  • The bends are not a detail. At a 200 nm gap they are worth 2.9 µm of straight coupler, a fifth of the device.
  • Do not extrapolate the fit far. It is fitted between 0 and 18 µm. It predicts full crossing at 32.9 µm; no run checks that.
  • The fields are not kept. A project with fields is hundreds of megabytes. The script reads the port results and saves the project again without them.

Step 3. The device, by FDTD #

The question Does the coupler, as the builder draws it, split 50:50 at 1.55 µm, and what does it do across the band?

The model. The layout that devices/directional_coupler_builder.py writes, with its 15 µm straight section, in the set-up of step 2. One run gives the whole band from 1.50 to 1.60 µm.

The results (coupler_fdtd.txt):

quantity value
cross, 1.55 µm 0.4986
through, 1.55 µm 0.5007
loss, 1.55 µm 0.003 dB
equal split at 1550.3 nm
back-reflection, 1.55 µm −43.5 dB
light into port 2, 1.55 µm −43.6 dB
the split moves by 0.32 percentage points per nm
between 45:55 and 55:45 from 1536 to 1565 nm
cross fraction at 1500 nm and 1600 nm 35 % and 67 %
loss, 1.50 to 1.60 µm 0.003 to 0.009 dB

Reading the figure.

  • a, the field. The light enters at the lower left. Along the straight section it moves into the upper waveguide, and at the right both waveguides carry the same.
  • b, the split. The two lines cross at 1550 nm. Away from it the split changes almost linearly, by a third of a percentage point per nanometre.
  • c, loss and reflection. The loss stays under 0.01 dB. The reflection, on the right axis, stays under −40 dB.

What it tells you. The device does what steps 1 and 2 designed it for: 49.9 % crosses at 1.55 µm, against the 49.9 % the fit predicts for 15.0 µm. It is a narrow-band splitter: the split stays inside 45:55 from 1536 to 1565 nm only. Where a split must hold over a wide band, the MMI splitter is the better device.

The mesh, and how well the length is known. The run was repeated with 10 nm over the gap instead of 20 nm (coupler_fdtd_fine.txt; 37 minutes instead of 13):

mesh over the gap cross at 1.55 µm through equal split at loss
20 nm, the default 0.4986 0.5007 1550.3 nm 0.003 dB
10 nm 0.4855 0.5137 1554.3 nm 0.004 dB

The finer mesh couples 2.6 % less. The equal split moves by 4 nm, and at 1.55 µm the device then splits 48.6:51.4. By the sin² law of step 2, the straight section would have to be about 0.3 µm longer, 15.3 µm, to bring the equal split back to 1550 nm. That figure is an estimate, not a run.

So the 50:50 length is known to about 0.3 µm, and the 15.0 µm of the builder is the answer of the default mesh. The finer mesh moves the FDTD towards step 1, whose Lπ was the longer one. Running step 2 again with --mesh 0.01 takes about two hours and settles it; it is exercise 4.

Limits #

  • Smooth, vertical sidewalls, and the gap exactly as drawn. In fabrication the gap is the dimension that varies; step 1 gives its effect on Lπ.
  • TE light only.
  • The coupler is simulated alone. In a circuit, the waveguides that lead to it add their own loss and phase.
  • The numbers are for a teaching process and have not been compared with a fabricated device.

Exercises #

  1. A tap. Use the fit of step 2 to choose the length for a 10 % tap. Build it, simulate it with coupler_fdtd.py after changing LENGTH in the builder, and compare.
  2. A wider gap. Step 1 gives Lπ = 90 µm at a 300 nm gap. Run step 2 with the builder's GAP at 0.30 and lengths of 0, 15, 30 and 45 µm. Are the bends worth more or less than at 200 nm, and why?
  3. The wavelength of equal split. By how much must the length change to move the equal split from 1550 to 1530 nm? Estimate it from panel b of figure 2, then run it.
  4. The finer mesh. Run coupling_length_fdtd.py --mesh 0.01. What are Lπ, Lbends and the 50:50 length now, and how close is Lπ to the 37.55 µm of step 1? Set the builder's LENGTH to the new value and run coupler_fdtd.py --mesh 0.01 --name coupler_fdtd_fine again.
