A thermo-optic phase shifter is a waveguide with a heater above it. A current through the heater warms the silicon core, warm silicon has a higher refractive index, and the light that passes gains phase. It is the tuning element of interferometers, switches and filters. A mesh of Mach-Zehnder interferometers for optical computing has two of them in every interferometer.

A heater has two numbers that matter: the power it needs for a phase shift of π, and the time it takes to get there. This chapter draws a phase shifter with the PDK and finds both. Its design choice is a pair of deep trenches beside the heater. They stop the heat from spreading sideways, so the same phase needs less than half the power. The chapter also shows what the trenches cost in speed, and what a waveguide next to the heater still feels.

The work takes four steps: the heat first, then the phase, then the speed, then the neighbour.

The device #

devices/thermo_optic_phase_shifter_builder.py draws a strip waveguide, 0.5 µm wide, with a film of titanium nitride (TiN) above it. The film is 150 nm thick and lies 0.88 µm above the core. At each end it widens into a landing, where a via takes the current up to metal 2, and a wire leads north to a pad. A deep trench runs along each side of the film. From the repository root:

shell
uv run python devices/thermo_optic_phase_shifter_builder.py

heater 100 um long, trenches along 96 um (96% of the filament), filament resistance 500 ohm
GDS -> devices/gds/thermo_optic_phase_shifter.gds   ports: ['o1', 'o2', 'H1', 'H2']
DRC passed: 0 errors, 0 warnings
parameter default where it comes from
length 100 µm the heated length: the film between its two landings. It sets the temperature and the voltage, not the power (step 2)
heater_width 2.0 µm the widest film that the closest trench allows (step 1)
trench_gap 2.0 µm centre line of the core to the inner edge of each trench: the closest the rules allow. None draws no trenches
trench_width 6.0 µm the width does not enter the result (step 1); the minimum of the layer is 5 µm
width 0.5 µm the strip of the strip waveguide chapter

Light enters at o1 and leaves at o2. The current flows from pad H1 to pad H2. The film between the landings has a resistance of 10 Ω/sq × 100 µm / 2 µm = 500 Ω.

The trenches. The layer DEEPTRENCH removes everything from the top of the cladding down to 5 µm inside the substrate, and leaves air. The two trenches leave a wall of oxide, 4 µm wide, with the core and the film in it. Three rules of the PDK meet at a gap of 2.0 µm:

rule value what the device has
minimum space of DEEPTRENCH: the wall between two trenches 4.00 µm 4.00 µm
trench_to_heater: trench to the edge of the film 1.00 µm 1.00 µm
trench_to_waveguide: trench to the edge of the core (a warning) 1.50 µm 1.75 µm
trench_to_metal: trench to a metal 2.00 µm 2.00 µm to each wire

The last rule is why the trenches are 96 µm long and the film 100 µm: they stop 2 µm before the wires. All four are teaching values of this PDK, not foundry rules (docs/pdk.md). The builder refuses a set of arguments that breaks one of them, and its message names the rule.

The reference. trench_gap=None draws the same device without trenches. The pads, the wires and the film stay where they are. Every step simulates both, so each result of the trenches has a number to stand against.

No undercut. The process also has a layer UNDERCUT, which removes the substrate under a heater. This device does not use it, for two reasons. With a trench on each side and no substrate below, the wall hangs free, and its heat can leave only through its two ends. A cross-section does not contain the ends, so the model of this chapter cannot describe it (see the pitfalls of step 1). And an undercut is a different trade: the published devices need a milliwatt or so, and take from a hundred microseconds to two milliseconds (see Against published devices).

The steps #

step folder solver script the question it answers
1 1_heat_cross_section HEAT, steady state heat_cross_section.py How many kelvin does the core gain per milliwatt, with and without trenches?
2 2_phase_shift MODE (FDE) phase_shift_fde.py How much heater power gives a phase shift of π?
3 3_switching_time HEAT, transient heat_transient.py How fast does the core follow the power, and what do the trenches cost in speed?
4 4_neighbour HEAT, steady state neighbour_heat.py How much of the heat reaches a waveguide next to the heater?

The method of steps 1 and 2 is that of the Ansys application example "Thermally tuned waveguide": a HEAT run gives the temperature, the temperature map is laid over the optical model, and a mode solver gives the phase. Here both models are built from the layout and the process file.

Run it #

You need Lumerical with HEAT and MODE. See the prerequisites of chapter 4. The run times are from the author's machine, with Lumerical 2025 R1.

shell
cd lumerical_simulations/thermo_optic_phase_shifter/scripts
uv run python heat_cross_section.py     # step 1, about 12 minutes for 22 cases
uv run python phase_shift_fde.py        # step 2, about 2 minutes
uv run python heat_transient.py         # step 3, about 10 minutes
uv run python neighbour_heat.py         # step 4, about 3 minutes

Run them in this order. Step 2 reads the temperature maps that step 1 writes (device_T_10mW.mat and two more). Like the Lumerical projects, the maps are not kept in the repository, so step 2 needs a run of step 1 first. Steps 3 and 4 read the result arrays of steps 1 and 2, which are kept.

The figures are redrawn from the kept results, without Lumerical:

uv run python lumerical_simulations/thermo_optic_phase_shifter/figures/fig1_heat.py

Step 1. The heat, by HEAT #

The question How many kelvin does one milliwatt of heater power add to the core, and what do the trenches change?

The idea. In the steady state all the heat that the film makes leaves through the silicon substrate, which is the heat sink. Silicon conducts heat about a hundred times better than oxide: 148 against 1.38 W/m/K in the thermal models that the process file names for the two materials (step 4 reads both values back from the heat flux). So what decides the temperature is the oxide between the film and the substrate: 0.88 µm of cladding down to the core, then 2 µm of buried oxide. The rise of the core is the power times the thermal resistance of the path below it.

Without trenches the heat spreads sideways on its way down. The path gets wider, its resistance falls, and the core stays cooler. A trench on each side stops the spreading: all the heat has to go down through the wall. If it flowed evenly through a wall of width W and length L, the buried oxide of thickness t under the core would give

rise of the core per unit power = t / (k W L) = 2 µm / (1.38 W/m/K × 4 µm × 100 µm) = 3.62 K/mW

One consequence is worth stating before any run. The rise per milliwatt goes as 1 / L: a film twice as long warms half as much for the same power. The rise times the length, in K µm/mW, is a property of the cross-section alone. It is the number to compare two heaters by.

The model. A straight piece of the device's cross-section: the core, the film and, for the device, the two trenches. It is written to GDS and loaded into a Layer Builder with the process file, so the stack, the depth of the trench and the materials come from there. The run is a two-dimensional steady state normalised to the heated length of 100 µm, so the power that is set is the total heater power. The region is 40 µm wide and ends 0.2 µm below the top of the cladding. The mesh has edges of 0.02 to 0.5 µm.

The heat source is a uniform source on the solid Heater, and the heat sink a temperature of 300 K on the solid Si_Substrate. Chapter 4 explains why the source has to be written this way. The recipe is add_heat_cross_section in solvers.py.

The script solves 22 cases: the device and the reference, each at 5, 10, 20 and 40 mW; six more trench gaps; three more heater widths, each with and without trenches; and the checks.

Reading the figure.

  • a, without trenches. The heat spreads over some 10 µm to each side on its way down. The film reaches 30 K.
  • b, with trenches. The wall is the only way down. Above the film the oxide is as hot as the film, because no heat flows upwards: the top of the oxide is insulating in this model. Below the film the temperature falls evenly to the substrate.
  • c, the gap. At the closest gap the simulated rise lies 7 % above the even wall. With a wider gap the two part: the even wall keeps falling as 1 / W, and the simulated rise levels off at the value without trenches. A trench 10 µm away adds 2 %.
  • d, the power. The four lines are straight. The largest deviation from the slope is 0.001 %.

What it tells you. A trench has to be close to work. At 2 µm it multiplies the rise by 2.19, at 4 µm by 1.33 and at 6 µm by 1.12. Step 4 gives the scale: without trenches the rise falls off sideways by a factor e every 4.8 µm, so a trench further out than that finds little heat to stop. The width of the trench does not matter: 4, 6 and 10 µm give the same rise.

The film width is the smaller lever. A narrower film heats the core 2 % more, but it runs hotter itself and has twice the resistance. A 3 µm film pushes the trench out to 2.5 µm, by the rule trench_to_heater, and that costs 19 %. So the device takes the widest film that the closest trench allows.

The film is always hotter than the core, here by a factor 1.5. It is the film that limits how far the phase can be driven. Step 2 gives its temperature at π and at 2π.

Checks.

  • Energy. The heat that arrives at the sink equals the power that was set within 0.03 % in all 22 cases.
  • Width of the region. With trenches the result is the same for 20, 40 and 80 µm. Without them a 20 µm region is 2 % high, and 40 µm agrees with 80 µm within 0.03 %.
  • Mesh. With the edges halved the rise changes by +0.23 % (device) and +0.25 % (reference).
  • By hand. The even wall gives 362 K µm/mW, the simulation 387, which is 7 % more. The film is 2 µm wide and the wall 4 µm, so under the film the heat is denser than at the edges of the wall, and the core lies under the film.

Pitfalls.

  • The air is not in the model. HEAT solves the solids. The trench is a hole with insulating walls: nothing conducts or convects across it. For a deep trench that is a fair model, since air conducts some fifty times less than oxide (0.026 W/m/K, a textbook value that is not in the process file).
  • A trench together with an undercut does not converge, and the script is not told. A trial for this chapter put the UNDERCUT layer under the wall (Lumerical 2025 R1; the run is not kept). The wall then has no solid path to the heat sink. The solver log says "Heat Flow Newton solver failed to converge after 40 iterations … a partial result will be saved", the call from Python raises nothing, and the temperature that is read back is −4000 K everywhere. The scripts therefore run the solver through run_checked in solvers.py, which reads the log <project>_p0.log after every run and stops when the solver did not converge. An undercut alone, without trenches, converges: the oxide then still holds on at its sides.
  • Compare heaters by K µm/mW. The rise per milliwatt of a 50 µm heater is twice that of a 100 µm heater of the same cross-section. Neither is the better heater.

Step 2. The phase, by MODE #

The question How much heater power gives a phase shift of π?

The idea. After a length L, light with the effective index neff has the phase 2π neff L / λ. A change of the index by Δneff changes the phase by 2π L Δneff / λ. That is π when Δneff = λ / (2 L), here 1.55 µm / 200 µm = 0.00775. The index follows the power linearly, so

P_π = λ / (2 L · dn_eff/dP)

where P is the total heater power. Step 1 showed that the rise per milliwatt goes as 1 / L. So does dn_eff/dP, and the length cancels: P_π does not depend on the length of the heater. A film twice as long needs half the rise for the same phase, and reaches it with the same power. What the length sets is the temperature at π, and with the resistance the voltage.

The model. The cross-section of step 1 in the mode solver. The silicon of the process file has a thermo-optic coefficient of 1.87 × 10⁻⁴ /K, so its index follows the temperature once the simulation has a temperature map. The script lays the maps of step 1 over the structure one at a time, as a temperature grid attribute, and finds the TE0 mode at 1.55 µm: cold, at 10 mW and at 20 mW for the device, cold and at 10 mW for the reference.

The window is 3.4 µm by 1.8 µm with PML on four sides. It holds silicon and oxide only: it ends inside the wall, before the trench, and below the film. The mesh is 20 nm, with 10 nm and a mesh line on every surface of the core.

Reading the figure.

  • a, the mode. 68 % of |E|² lies in the core. Weighted with the permittivity that is 92 % of the electric energy, and the energy is what counts for the change of the index.
  • b, the power. The two points with trenches lie on one line within 0.05 %. The line reaches π at 10.0 mW and the line without trenches at 22.0 mW.
  • c, the wall. The change falls about sixty times for every 0.25 µm. That is close to the square of the tail of the mode in the oxide, exp(−2γd) with γ = 8.0 µm⁻¹, which gives 55 times. At the 1.75 µm of the device nothing is left of it.

What it tells you. The device needs 10.26 mW for π, and without trenches it would need 22.0 mW. A circuit that must reach every phase needs 2π: 20.5 mW at 3.2 V, with the film 118 K above the substrate.

The core has to warm by 38.9 K for π, whatever the trenches do. That number follows from the length alone: λ / (2 L) divided by the change of neff per kelvin.

The effective index changes by 1.994 × 10⁻⁴ per kelvin, 7 % more than the index of silicon itself. Only 92 % of the energy is in the silicon, which would make it less. But the light is slow: its group index is 4.24 against 3.48 for silicon, so it spends more time in the heated silicon than a plane wave would. The product, (4.24 / 3.48) × 0.92 × 1.87 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.10 × 10⁻⁴ /K, is 5 % above the simulated value; it leaves out the dispersion of silicon.

A trench wall 1.75 µm from the core does not touch the light. The rule of 1.5 µm leaves room for a real wall, which is rough, where this one is perfectly flat.

The film is 0.88 µm above the core. In a window that holds it, it adds 0.0085 dB/cm, which is 0.0001 dB over the device.

Checks.

  • Mesh. With the mesh halved the cold index moves by 9 × 10⁻⁴, and the change per milliwatt by 0.10 %. The error of the mesh is the same in the cold and in the warm run and cancels in the difference.
  • Window. A window 5.0 µm wide gives the same change per milliwatt for the reference.
  • Against the strip chapter. The cold mode has neff = 2.44449 and ng = 4.2387. The strip waveguide chapter has 2.44448 and 4.2387 for the same guide in another window.
  • Linearity. The runs at 10 and 20 mW give the same change per milliwatt within 0.05 %.

Pitfalls.

  • The path of the temperature file. The script command importdataset reads a backslash followed by a t as a tab. On Windows a path through \thermo_optic_phase_shifter therefore fails with "cannot open file". The script passes the path with forward slashes.
  • Keep the trench and the film out of the window when they are not the question. For P_π the window holds silicon and oxide only. The wall and the film each get a window of their own, and each is compared with a second run in the same window. The absolute loss of such a window is not a property of the guide: the low window of the wall check reports 0.0055 dB/cm for the guide without trenches too, from the boundary above and below the core.
  • The oxide has no thermo-optic coefficient in the process file. With a textbook value of about 1 × 10⁻⁵ /K, the 8 % of the energy outside the silicon would add about one per cent to the change of the index, by the same estimate as above: (4.24 / 1.44) × 0.08 × 1 × 10⁻⁵ /K.

Step 3. The speed, by HEAT #

The question How fast does the core follow the heater power, and what do the trenches cost in speed?

The idea. Before the core settles, the oxide around it has to warm up. Without trenches the oxide near the core warms first and quickly, and the oxide further out adds little. With trenches the whole wall has to warm, from the substrate to the top of the cladding. The wall is a column of oxide that is cold at its foot and insulated everywhere else. Its slowest mode decays with

τ_slowest = 4 h² / (π² α) = 25.8 µs      h = 7.5 µm, the height of the column in the model
                                         α = k / (ρ c) = 8.84 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s for fused silica

The model. The cross-section of step 1 in a transient run. The run starts cold, and 10 mW are switched on at t = 0. The mean temperature of the core is read at every time step, which the solver chooses between 20 ns and 2 µs. For the device the power goes off again after 400 µs, so that the fall is simulated too. The same is run for trench gaps of 3, 4 and 6 µm and without trenches.

The results (switching.txt):

The table compares cross-sections. Its 10.0 mW at 2 µm is the cross-section with a trench on each side, not the 10.26 mW of the device as drawn. The power for π of a gap is that value of step 2, scaled by the rise of the core that step 1 found for the gap. For the guide without trenches the scaling gives 21.98 mW, and step 2 solved that case: 22.00 mW.

Reading the figure.

  • a, on and off. The film jumps and the core follows. The fall mirrors the rise, as it must in a linear model.
  • b, the gap. Every curve starts steeply and ends slowly. The closer the trench, the more of the rise is left to the slow end.
  • c, power against time. From no trenches to the closest trench the points move down and to the right: less power, more time. None of them moves towards the lower left, where a better heater would lie.

What it tells you. In the cross-section the trenches lower the power by a factor 2.19 and raise the time constant by a factor 2.94. The product of the two is the figure of merit that papers quote, and smaller is better. It is larger with any of these trenches than without: 135 mW µs without, 181 with the closest one, and up to 224 in between. The heat that the trench keeps in has to fill the wall first.

So the trench is the right choice where power counts and a few kilohertz are enough: a mesh that is set and then left alone. It is the wrong one where the phase has to move fast.

Checks.

  • Final value. The rise ends within 0.01 % of the steady state of step 1.
  • Time step. With half the time step the time constant and the rise time move by 0.02 %.
  • Length of the run. A run twice as long ends within 0.02 % of the same value.
  • Rise against fall. The 10 to 90 % times agree within 0.5 %. The time constant of the fall is 3.8 % longer than that of the rise, and 1.9 % with half the time step: the solver starts the rise with its smallest step and meets the switch-off with its largest.
  • By hand. The late part of the simulated rise, from 60 to 150 µs, decays with 25.3 µs. The slowest mode of the column gives 25.8 µs, 2 % more. The other gaps give 25.4 to 25.6 µs: the same column, whatever its width. The time constant of 18.0 µs is shorter because the faster modes carry part of the rise.

Pitfalls.

  • One number is not the response. A single exponential has a rise time of 2.20 time constants. The device has 2.77 and the guide without trenches 4.39, because heat diffuses: it starts fast and ends slowly. A time constant, a 10 to 90 % time and a −3 dB bandwidth are three different quantities. Say which one you quote.
  • The switch-off meets a large time step. If the fall is what you need, lower the largest time step, as the check does.
  • The time steps make the project large. The solver stores every step in the project file. The script switches back to layout mode before it saves.

Step 4. The neighbour, by HEAT #

The question How much of the heat reaches a waveguide next to the heater?

The idea. In a circuit the neighbours of a heated waveguide are other waveguides whose phase matters. A neighbour that warms by 1 % of the heated core's own rise takes 1 % of its phase, unasked, if it is as long as the heater and lies beside it.

Heat reaches a neighbour on two paths. One goes sideways through the oxide. The other goes down into the silicon substrate, along it, and up again. A trench cuts the first. It cannot cut the second: it reaches 5 µm into a substrate that is hundreds of micrometres thick.

The model. Three parts.

  • a. The cross-section of step 1 in a region 120 µm wide, with the substrate held at 300 K as in steps 1 to 3. Only the path through the oxide exists.
  • b. The same with the substrate in the model: 9.5 µm of silicon, and the bottom of the simulation region held at 300 K instead of the whole solid. Heat can now pass under the trenches.
  • c. An estimate by formula, not a simulation, for a wafer 725 µm thick. That thickness is the usual one of a 200 mm wafer and an assumption of this chapter, not a value of the process. A heater 100 µm long is short against such a wafer, so its heat spreads in three dimensions, which a cross-section cannot show.

With the substrate in the model the heated core itself is 3.2 % warmer with trenches and 1.6 % without. That is what steps 1 to 3 leave out by holding the substrate at 300 K.

Reading the figure.

  • a, the oxide. Without trenches the rise falls by a factor e every 4.8 µm. With trenches and the substrate held at 300 K nothing is left beyond them. With the substrate in the model a little heat passes under them.
  • b, the wafer. The path through the oxide falls steeply. The estimate for the wafer falls about as 1 / d. From about 20 µm on it is the larger of the two, also without trenches.

What it tells you. The trenches remove the path through the oxide. Without them a neighbour 20 µm away takes 1.0 % of the phase, and one 30 µm away 0.13 %.

The path through the substrate remains, with or without trenches. By the estimate a neighbour 50 µm away takes 0.4 % of the phase, and distance helps slowly: 0.2 % at 100 µm. At P_π it warms by 0.17 K. In a circuit with many heaters these shares add up. The phases then cannot be set one by one: each has to be set with the others at their working power.

Checks.

  • The materials. From the heat flux between two planes, the buried oxide conducts 1.380 W/m/K and the substrate 148.0 W/m/K in these runs.
  • The oxide, by hand. A layer of oxide of height h, cold at its foot and insulated on top, passes a rise sideways as exp(−π d / (2 h)). For the 7.5 µm of the model that is a factor e every 4.77 µm; the fit of run a, from 15 to 50 µm, gives 4.78 µm.
  • The formula for a slab. Before it is used for the wafer, the slab formula is tested on run b: summed over the strip where the oxide hands its heat to the substrate, it gives the simulated rise of the substrate surface within 0.2 % from 10 to 40 µm.
  • The estimate c itself is not checked by a simulation. It needs a three-dimensional model.

Pitfalls.

  • A substrate held at 300 K hides the path through it. It is the usual heat sink of a cross-section, and steps 1 to 3 use it. For the heated core it costs 3 %. For a neighbour it hides everything.
  • To put the substrate in the model, hold the bottom of the region. The temperature boundary condition then has the surface type "simulation region" with z min switched on, instead of the solid Si_Substrate.
  • A thin substrate is not a wafer. Run b has 9.5 µm of silicon over a cold plane, which carries heat sideways over a few micrometres only: 0.02 % at 10 µm. In a wafer the heat has 725 µm to spread in, and the estimate is twenty times larger at five times the distance.

Against published devices #

The numbers above are simulations of a teaching process. No measured device of exactly this kind, a TiN film above a 220 nm strip with trenches and no undercut, was found to compare them with. The published devices below are of other kinds. They show where a heater of this kind stands.

device power speed source
this chapter: TiN film above the core, with trenches (simulated) 10.3 mW for π τ = 18.0 µs steps 2 and 3
this chapter: the same without trenches (simulated) 22.0 mW for π τ = 6.1 µs steps 2 and 3
heater of doped silicon in the waveguide itself 24.77 mW for π 130 kHz (−3 dB) Harris et al. 2014
heater in the silicon of an adiabatic bend, about 10 µm long 12.7 mW for π τ = 2.4 µs Watts et al. 2013
phase shifter of an interferometer in a foundry process, without and with substrate undercut 43.46 mW and 1.07 mW for π rise time 12.6 µs and 1.853 ms Parsons et al. 2025
free-standing strip waveguides 0.54 mW to switch rise time 141 µs Sun and Reano 2010
suspended arms 0.49 mW to switch rise 144 µs, fall 122 µs Fang et al. 2011
racetrack resonator with undercut 2.4 mW per free spectral range 10 to 90 % in about 170 µs Dong et al. 2010

Three things follow from the table.

  1. Where the heat is made. Watts et al. make it in the silicon of the waveguide itself, and report a product of power and time constant of 30.5 mW µs. The film of this chapter has 135 mW µs without trenches. One difference is that its heat is made 0.88 µm above the core and has to cross the oxide first; the comparison is of one measured device with one simulated film.
  2. Isolation buys power with time. The measured devices with an undercut or free-standing waveguides need a milliwatt or so, and take from a hundred microseconds to two milliseconds. The trenches of this chapter are a small step in the same direction: half the power, three times the time.
  3. Trenches are also there for the neighbours. Jacques et al. conclude that deep trenches are the best structures that need no special fabrication against the heat that leaks to devices near a heater. Step 4 shows which path they close and which they leave open.

Limits #

  • The model is a cross-section. The device as drawn is the average of two cross-sections along its film. The ends of the film, the landings, the aluminium wires and the pads are not in the thermal model; the wires carry some heat away at both ends.
  • The air is not in the model, neither in the trenches nor above the chip. The top of the oxide is insulating, and the region ends 0.2 µm below it.
  • Steps 1 to 3 hold the substrate at 300 K. Step 4 puts that at 3 % of the core rise. A real chip sits on a carrier that warms too.
  • The material values are those of the process file at 300 K. At 2π the film is 118 K hotter than that, and the conductivity of a deposited film depends on how it was made. The linearity check tests the solver, not the materials.
  • The phase is that of TE0 at 1.55 µm. The oxide has no thermo-optic coefficient in the process file.
  • No undercut, and no three-dimensional run.
  • The numbers are for a teaching process and have not been compared with a fabricated device.

Check yourself #

  1. Why does the power for π not depend on the length of the heater? What does the length set?
  2. The trenches multiply the rise of the core by 2.19, but the device as drawn gains only 2.14 times the rise without trenches. Where does the difference come from?
  3. The even wall gives 3.62 K/mW and the simulation 3.87 K/mW. What does the formula leave out?
  4. The time constant is 18.0 µs and the 10 to 90 % rise time 49.9 µs. A single exponential would have a rise time of 2.20 time constants, or 39.5 µs. Why is the rise time longer?
  5. With trenches and the substrate held at 300 K, a neighbour 10 µm away feels nothing. Why is that not the crosstalk of a real chip?
Answers
  1. The phase needs a fixed product of length and index change. A longer film needs a smaller rise, and the rise per milliwatt of a longer film is smaller by the same factor, so the power is the same. The length sets the temperature at π, 38.9 K for 100 µm, and through the resistance the voltage.
  2. The trenches run beside 96 µm of the 100 µm of film, because they stop 2 µm before the metal wires. The last 2 µm at each end heat the core as a film without trenches does.
  3. It assumes that the heat is spread evenly over the 4 µm of the wall when it passes the core. The film is only 2 µm wide and 0.88 µm above the core, so the heat is still denser in the middle, where the core is.
  4. Heat diffuses. The rise is a sum of modes with different time constants: the fast ones give the steep start, and the slowest one, 25 µs for the wall, gives the long end.
  5. Holding the substrate at 300 K removes the path through it. In a wafer the heat spreads in the substrate and warms what stands on it. Step 4 estimates 0.4 % of the phase at 50 µm.

Exercises #

  1. A shorter heater. Set LENGTH in devices/thermo_optic_phase_shifter_builder.py to 50, and run steps 1 and 2. Compare the power for π, the rise of the core per milliwatt, the temperature of the film at π and the voltage with the tables above. Which of them change, and by how much? Copy the folders 1_heat_cross_section and 2_phase_shift first, because the runs overwrite their results. Afterwards restore them and set LENGTH back: the tests compare the kept layout with the builder.
  2. A faster device. A circuit needs a time constant below 10 µs. Read from the table of step 3 which trench gap that allows, and how much power for π it saves against no trenches. Then run uv run python heat_transient.py --gaps 2.5 10 from the scripts folder, for two gaps that step 1 has and the table of step 3 has not. Copy the folder 3_switching_time first, because the run overwrites its results, and restore it afterwards.
  3. When has the phase settled? Read device_t_s and device_core_K from 3_switching_time/switching.npz, and find the time after which the core stays within 1 % of its final value. How many time constants is that, and what does a single exponential predict?
  4. The film as the limit. Suppose the film may not get hotter than 150 K above the substrate. From the rises per milliwatt in 2_phase_shift/phase.txt, find the largest phase the device can reach, and the largest without trenches. Why do the trenches help here too?
  5. The pitch of two arms. Without trenches, how far apart must the two arms of an interferometer be for the path through the oxide to stay below 0.1 % of the phase? Use the table of step 4. What does the estimate for the wafer say at that distance?
